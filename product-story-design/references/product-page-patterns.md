## Core Shared Structure

Across product families, this style tends to reuse this logic:

1. Persistent global nav and a compact product-local nav
2. Hero with product name, short tagline, and primary buy action
3. Early summary of strongest selling points
4. Deeper sections organized by decision categories
5. Accessory, ecosystem, or compatibility sections near the lower half
6. Technical or legal detail separated from emotional storytelling

The page is both a brand experience and a buying aid.

## Four Product-Page Archetypes

### 1. Flagship Launch

Best example: iPhone 17 Pro.

Traits:

- strongest cinematic imagery
- premium finish and materials emphasized early
- highlights rail near the top
- `Take a closer look` object-viewer section
- more immersive camera/performance storytelling
- stronger emphasis on pro capability than price clarity

Use this archetype when:

- the product is aspirational
- industrial design is a major selling point
- multiple premium features deserve extended screen time

### 2. Value Launch

Best examples: iPhone 17e and iPad 11-inch.

Traits:

- quicker explanation of value proposition
- clearer, more direct claims
- more frequent mention of practical benefits
- more visible comparisons to prior or nearby products
- less cinematic indulgence, more "why this is worth it"

Use this archetype when:

- price sensitivity matters
- the story is feature density at approachable value
- the target user needs confidence more than spectacle

### 3. Creator Portable

Best examples: iPad Air and iPad mini.

Traits:

- balances performance, portability, and accessory workflow
- highlights chip, OS, and Pencil/Keyboard ecosystem together
- presents the device as a flexible creative/work surface
- sections often connect hardware with usage modes rather than isolated specs

Use this archetype when:

- the product is both a device and a workflow hub
- accessories materially change the use case
- portability is part of the emotional promise

### 4. Accessory Decision Page

Best example: a stylus accessory page.

Traits:

- starts with category-level promise
- quickly splits into model-specific sections
- includes compatibility lists
- ends with comparison matrix and selection guidance
- less cinematic than device pages, more decision-support utility

Use this archetype when:

- users must choose the right accessory
- compatibility is a major decision factor
- the story is capability plus fit

### 5. Performance Wearable

Best examples: value and rugged smartwatch pages.

Traits:

- starts with a short emotional line
- quickly moves into `Get the highlights`
- then expands into use-case chapters such as health, fitness, running, safety, or outdoors
- blends lifestyle imagery with close-up hardware views
- treats the device as both object and always-on companion

Use this archetype when:

- the product lives on the body
- features map naturally to real-life activity categories
- trust, readiness, and durability matter as much as beauty

### 6. New-Platform Manifesto

Best example: a spatial-computing launch page.

Traits:

- hero acts like a category-defining statement, not a normal launch hero
- typography becomes more cinematic and broken into short lines
- sections are organized around paradigm shifts like work, entertainment, photos, communication, and interface model
- imagery aims to explain a new interaction model, not just showcase industrial design

Use this archetype when:

- the product introduces a new computing model
- the audience must first understand the category before comparing features
- the page needs to feel historic, inevitable, and visionary

### 7. Sensory Experience Product

Best examples: AirPods 4, AirPods Pro 3, and AirPods Max 2.

Traits:

- hero emphasizes feeling and sound quality first
- sections translate invisible experiences into visual proof
- more emphasis on fit, seal, ANC, audio architecture, and comfort details
- frequent use of film, music, movement, and close-up industrial detail

Use this archetype when:

- the core benefit is auditory or sensory
- the product experience cannot be fully shown directly on screen
- comfort and technical tuning matter together

### 8. Co-Branded Variant Page

Best example: a co-branded watch variant page.

Traits:

- shorter page
- brand-collaboration framing
- one focused story about style, materials, or subculture fit
- more direct shopping intent

Use this archetype when:

- the product is a branded edition or collaboration
- differentiation is real but narrower than a full flagship launch
- the goal is affinity and quick merchandising, not a full platform story

### 9. Everyday Computing Device

Best examples: MacBook Air, MacBook Neo, and iMac.

Traits:

- balances object beauty with practical daily-use proof
- highlights often appear very early
- color, thinness, portability, or room presence matter strongly
- platform and continuity features help reduce buyer friction

Use this archetype when:

- the product is a daily driver
- buyers care about feel, ease, and trust more than extreme pro workflows
- the page needs to blend emotional appeal with approachable proof

### 10. Workstation Computing Flagship

Best example: MacBook Pro.

Traits:

- premium and serious tone
- stronger performance proof and comparison content
- more time spent on chip families, battery, display, and pro use cases
- object beauty remains important, but hard evidence occupies more of the page

Use this archetype when:

- the buyer needs both desire and justification
- multiple performance tiers matter
- the page must support a more demanding purchase decision

## Repeating Section Patterns

### Hero Pattern

Observed across all pages:

- product name
- ultra-short tagline or stacked lines
- one main buy CTA
- one strong product visual

Common forms:

- `Feature stacked. Value packed.`
- `Whoosh.`
- `Lovable. Drawable. Magical.`
- `Dream it up. Jot it down.`

The hero usually says very little and lets the object carry the emotional load.

### Highlights Pattern

Observed on iPhone 17e, iPhone 17 Pro, and iPad Air.

Traits:

- title like `Get the highlights.`
- optional `Watch the film`
- short chips or topics
- each highlight paired with one visual and one proof statement

Use when the product has multiple strong selling points and the page needs a quick preview before deep-dives.

### Design/Closer Look Pattern

Observed strongly on iPhone 17 Pro and design-led hardware pages.

Traits:

- large renders
- finishes/colors
- material or silhouette emphasis
- very low copy density
- optional `View in your space`

Use when physical form is a primary differentiator.

### Feature Deep-Dive Pattern

Observed across all device pages.

Typical buckets:

- design
- chip/performance
- camera
- OS/intelligence
- accessories

Each deep-dive usually focuses on one major decision category rather than mixing claims.

For wearables, these categories often become activity chapters:

- health
- fitness
- running
- safety
- outdoors

For sensory products, they often become experience chapters:

- fit
- noise control
- sound architecture
- comfort
- case or charging system

For new-platform pages, they often become worldview chapters:

- interface model
- productivity
- entertainment
- communication
- memories or media

For computing pages, they often become workflow chapters:

- design and portability
- chip and speed
- battery
- display
- camera and calls
- continuity or ecosystem
- switching or comparison

### Ecosystem Pattern

Observed on iPad and iPhone pages.

Traits:

- shows related accessories or software experiences
- reinforces that the product becomes more powerful inside a family
- usually appears after core hardware proof

Use when the ecosystem is part of the product value.

### Comparison/Compatibility Pattern

Observed strongly on stylus accessory pages and some lower-tier value pages.

Traits:

- practical, low-drama section
- factual rows and columns
- helps users choose or upgrade

Use when the buyer's next question is `Which one is right for me?`

### Film-Led Promo Pattern

Observed on AirPods 4 and some other entertainment-adjacent pages.

Traits:

- early CTA such as `Watch the film`
- campaign-style visual identity
- emotional lifestyle framing before or alongside technical proof

Use when the product benefits from a cultural or cinematic hook.

## Copy Logic By Product Type

### Flagship Pages

- more evocative
- fewer words
- more room for image-led beats
- confidence comes from restraint

### Value Pages

- more explicit benefit language
- more practical proof
- stronger mention of storage, battery, durability, price logic, or comparisons

### Creator Pages

- mixes aspiration with workflow clarity
- often ties hardware to apps, intelligence features, keyboard, and pen use

### Accessory Pages

- starts emotionally, then becomes highly utilitarian
- feature names become explicit and scannable

### Wearable Pages

- short and punchy hero language
- heavier use of second-person practical benefits
- feature sections tied to moments of daily life, exercise, safety, and health

### Sensory Pages

- more sensory adjectives and emotional framing
- invisible technical benefits are translated into physical metaphors or cinematic visuals

### New-Platform Pages

- language alternates between visionary and explanatory
- copy often introduces a new mental model before naming features

### Computing Pages

- balance emotional object language with practical workflow proof
- often use friendlier claims for everyday products and more serious proof for pro machines
- rely more on in-device UI imagery to sell the experience

## What Stays Constant

No matter the product family, these patterns repeat:

- Headline density stays low.
- CTAs stay simple and limited.
- Product imagery leads.
- Sections are decision-oriented, not random.
- Emotional storytelling sits above technical detail.
- Fine print and spec depth are separated from the main narrative.

## Extra Notes By Family

### Smartwatch Family

Watch pages are among the clearest examples of sectioning by life context rather than component spec. The device is sold as:

- a health sensor
- a fitness coach
- a safety tool
- a communication device
- a personal object with finishes and bands

This makes Watch pages excellent references when building pages for products that stay with the user all day.

### AirPods

Audio-product pages solve a hard design problem: audio is invisible. Strong examples compensate by using:

- cinematic lifestyle imagery
- enlarged hardware close-ups
- concise technical proof
- comfort and fit storytelling
- simple comparison and buy flows

These are ideal references for any product where the main benefit is felt more than seen.

### Spatial Computing Category

Vision Pro is less like a normal product page and more like a category introduction. It is useful when the skill needs to sell:

- a new interface model
- a futuristic workflow
- a platform shift

It uses very short, staged lines and stronger manifesto energy than the rest of these hardware launch pages.

### Co-Branded Watch Variant

Nike is a good reference for shorter collaboration pages:

- fewer sections
- stronger brand-partner framing
- faster path to shopping
- style and identity over deep technical education

### Mac Family

Mac product pages are especially useful references for:

- thinness staging
- color-system presentation
- balancing industrial design with workflow proof
- using software screenshots inside hardware renders to sell capability

They also show the clearest contrast between approachable computing pages like MacBook Air or iMac and evidence-heavy workstation pages like MacBook Pro.

## Implementation Advice For This Skill

When building a premium product page:

1. Pick the archetype first.
2. Define the top buying question the page must answer.
3. Organize sections by decision category, not by internal team structure.
4. Match copy density to product tier.
5. Use large visuals for premium proof and quieter tables for practical decisions.
6. If the benefit is invisible, translate it with imagery, metaphor, motion, or lifestyle proof.
7. If the product changes a user's behavior or worldview, organize the page around that shift, not around specs.
8. For computing products, decide early whether the page is selling daily ease or professional headroom. That choice changes the whole proof strategy.

If every product gets the same page skeleton, the skill is being used incorrectly.
